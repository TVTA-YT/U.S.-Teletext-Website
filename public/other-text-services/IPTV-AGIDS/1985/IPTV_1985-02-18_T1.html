today opened mostly steady in

Friday's gains in Chicago 

futures.

the U.S. department of Agricul-

  OVER 900 ATTEND HEARING ON

 
         FARM POLICY

 
================================

  A crowd estimated at over

900 crammed the American Legion

Hall in the midwest farming

community of Atchison, Kansas

last Friday to hear the house 

budget committee gather testi-

mony on farm programs in the 

l986 federal budget.

  Committee members said the 

crowd was a graphic indication 

of the deep concern that farmers

have over the future of their

troubled industry.  It was clear

that few favored the farm pro-

gram cuts proposed by President 

Reagan in his budget proposal.

  Kansas Governor John Carlin

said, "At stake here is not just

the fate of the three per cent 

of our population living on the

farm, but the fate of 21 per

cent of all Americans employed

in some phase of the agricul-

tural industry.

  Witnesses were almost unani-

mous in telling the committe 

that the farm economy was too 

weak to withstand the sweeping

farm program reforms proposed

in the budget.

They called for short term

measures to help credit-strapped

farmers plant a crop this 

spring, and urged across the

board spending cuts to erode

the huge federal deficit

which keeps the American dollar

strong and inhibits foreign

sales of U.S. farm products.

  Those cuts should include

reductions in military spending,

they said.

  B.L.Flinchbaugh, farm

economist at Kansas State Uni-

versity, drew cheers when he

said the President "has simply 

got to get off this kick of 

giving the military a free

check."

  He said a Kansas state poll

of 1,000 farmers showed two-

thirds willing to absorb farm

program cuts if other federal 

programs were reduced accord-

ingly.

  Jan Flora, a Kansas State

University Sociologist,testified

that research shows that middle-

sized farms provide a more

vibrant economic base for rural

communities than large farms

do, and argued for programs to

ease small and mid-sized farmers

through the current credit 

crisis.

  The Reagan administration

has enacted measures allowing

Federal guarantees for troubled

farmers home administration loan

if banks write down principal

or interest on the loans, but

witnesses said that was too

little too late.

  Eugene Hegarty, a farm banker

from Effingham, Kansas said

FMHA field offices do not have

the personnel to process credit

aid by the spring, when many

farmers must borrow again.

  Hegarty called for more FMHA

manpower and a long-term program

of federal loan guarantees for

farmers.

  Jim Allen, Chairman of the 

Kansas Legislatures Senate

Agriculture Committee, said his

group vors radical federal 

farm program reforms which 

would include a voluntary 

domestic production pool to 

limit production and control 

stocks.

  Such a program would restore

the competitive posture of U.S.

farm products on the world mar-

ket, and could be paid for by a 

production tax, a processors tax

and federal funds.

PLANTINGS INTENTIONS WILL MAKE

 
BEAN PRICES WEATHER-SENSITIVE

 
-------------------------------

 
 by Bob Wisner your I.S.U.

 
 Extention Outlook Economist


   News from USDA's February 15

planting intentions report may

slightly strengthen new-crop 

soybean prices in the next few

days and should make 1985 soy-

bean crop prices moderately 

sensitive to weather develop-

ments this spring and summer.

 At the same time, intended corn

plantings may place slight down-

ward pressure on new-crop corn

contracting prices for next fall

and winter.

   The USDA survey of farmers in

all major producing states indi-

cated that soybean growers ten-

tatively plan to reduce their

1985 soybean plantings 5 % below

last year's level.  That would 

leave U.S. planted soybean acre-

age at 64.4 mln. acres, down 3.3

mln. acres from 1984 and down 

6.5 mln. acres from 1982.  

   Intended U.S. corn plantings

were reported at 82 mln.acres --

approximately the same as in 

1982 and up 2 % from last year.

Any downward pressure on new-

crop corn prices as a result of

the report, however, should be

tempered by indications that 

participation in the 1985 feed

grain program will be heavy.

Program participation will pro-

vide farmers with ASCS price 

support loans as an alternative

to harvest sales as a means of

obtaining cash for fall 

expenses.

   Much of the decrease in in-

tended soybean plantings was in

the South, where farmers intend

to continue a recent shift from

beans to grain sorghum.  In ad-

dition, intended corn plantings

in several southern states were

up sharply from last year, ap-

parently at the expense of soy-

bean plantings.

    Increased corn plantings may

partly reflect the fact that wet

weather in some areas last year

prevented farmers from planting

all of their intended acreage.

Also, the $3.03 target price of-

fers farmers in the farm program

a potential return over variable

costs for corn that runs as much

as $50 to $70 per acre greater

than for growing soybeans.

   Iowa farmers indicated they 

plan to boost corn plantings 2 %

above last year, and to decrease

soybean plantings 1 % below the

1984 level.  Similar percentage

changes were indicated for most

other Corn Belt states.

   Intended U.S. sorghum plant-

ings were up 4 % from last year

along with a 5 % increase in in-

tended oat plantings. Sunflower-

seed growers indicated they in-

tend to plant 18 % fewer acres 

than last year, which should be

a slightly positive influence on

the soybean market.

   As usual,several factors will

determine whether these inten-

tions are carried out as report-

ed, including weather and price

trends this spring.  This year

the availability of financing to

meet planting expenses is an ad-

ditional factor that could alter

final plantings.  In some cases,

inadequate financing might tend

to shift extra acres from corn 

into soybeans or might encourage

marginal land to be left idle.
