          TENSIOMETER:

      
      

      
      


      
      

      
      
 Soil Moisture Measurement for

      
      

      
      


      
      

      
      
     Irrigation Management

      
      

      
      

      
      


      
      


      
      
A "mechanical root" may help

      
      


      
      
farmers irrigate their fields

      
      


      
      
efficiently.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
The mechanical root is

      
      


      
      
technically called a

      
      


      
      
tensiometer, said Richard

      
      


      
      
Warner, Extension agriculture

      
      


      
      
engineer with the University of

      
      


      
      
Kentucky.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
"A tensiometer is an indicator

      
      


      
      
of moisture tension in the soil.

      
      


      
      
Although it does not actually

      
      


      
      
measure the amount of water in

      
      


      
      
the soil, it does measure the

      
      


      
      
relative ease of the water

      
      


      
      
movement to the plant root," he

      
      


      
      
said.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
Warner said when the soil is

      
      


      
      
dry, moisture tension increases

      
      


      
      
and the water movement to the

      
      


      
      
plant is restricted.  He added

      
      


      
      
that water tension measured by

      
      


      
      
the tensiometer is directly

      
      


      
      
related to the moisture

      
      

      
      
available to the plant.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
"In a way we consider the

      
      


      
      
tensiometer a mechanical root

      
      


      
      
because it responds much like a

      
      


      
      
crop's root does to water

      
      


      
      
stress," Warner said.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
He said a vacuum gauge on the

      
      


      
      
tensiometer shows a low reading

      
      


      
      
when the soil is wet and a high

      
      


      
      
reading when the soil is dry.

      
      


      
      
The important factor is to learn

      
      


      
      
how to read the gauge for the

      
      


      
      
specific soil used.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
By learning to use a tensiometer

      
      


      
      
a farmer can learn when water is

      
      


      
      
needed and can avoid wilting and

      
      


      
      
stress in crops that can inhibit

      
      


      
      
an overall yield.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
Warner said the best place for

      
      


      
      
the tensiometer is a

      
      


      
      
representative location deep

      
      


      
      
enough to read the moisture

      
      


      
      
level where the root of the crop

      
      


      
      
is located.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
He said the tensiometer costs

      
      


      
      
about $45 and an additional $45

      
      


      
      
to install it.

        VEGETABLE CROPS

      
      

      
      

      
      


      
      


      
      
Vegetable processing companies,

      
      


      
      
marketing co-ops, local

      
      


      
      
wholesale outlets and retail

      
      


      
      
sales through farmer's markets

      
      


      
      
and road-side stands all offer

      
      


      
      
promises for extra dollars for

      
      


      
      
Kentucky farmers interested in

      
      


      
      
pursuing alternative income

      
      


      
      
sources.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
Vegetable processing companies

      
      


      
      
are in the process of signing

      
      


      
      
contracts now on bell peppers,

      
      


      
      
cucumbers for pickles and

      
      


      
      
tomatos, said Dick Roberts,

      
      


      
      
Extension vegetables specialist

      
      


      
      
with the University of Kentucky

      
      


      
      
College of Agriculture.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
In addition, those who are

      
      


      
      
interested in marketing their

      
      


      
      
vegetables through vegetable

      
      


      
      
co-ops should be making their

      
      


      
      
contacts now, Roberts said.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
Farmers can profit from growing

      
      


      
      
vegetables as a supplement to

      
      


      
      
other farm enterprises, Roberts

      
      


      
      
said, and 1988 has brought some

      
      


      
      
new opportunities for developing

      
      


      
      
vegetable crops.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
"There are vegetable co-ops over

      
      


      
      
the state that are looking for

      
      


      
      
increased crop acreage," Roberts

      
      


      
      
said.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
He said that this is an

      
      


      
      
opportunity for people who live

      
      


      
      
close to the co-ops to market

      
      


      
      
their vegetables.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
Another opportunity for the

      
      


      
      
producer is to make private

      
      


      
      
contacts with local

      
      


      
      
supermarkets, restaurants,

      
      


      
      
outlets and independent grocery

      
      


      
      
stores to sell their produce

      
      


      
      
wholesale, he said.  However,

      
      


      
      
the farmer should be aware that

      
      


      
      
if he has contracted with a

      
      


      
      
processing company or a co-op to

      
      


      
      
market a specified number of

      
      


      
      
acres, all produce from those

      
      


      
      
acres must go to the contractor.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
Those who may have additional

      
      


      
      
acres of vegetables, however,

      
      


      
      
will find that many businesses

      
      


      
      
are willing to buy from local

      
      


      
      
people, he said.  In addition,

      
      


      
      
many cities have farmer's  

      
      


      
      
markets to help market fresh

      
      


      
      
produce, and some farmers may

      
      


      
      
consider setting up a road-side

      
      


      
      
stand to sell their extra

      
      


      
      
produce at retail.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
To ensure growing good vegetable

      
      


      
      
crops farmers should have a soil

      
      


      
      
test done, prepare the soil

      
      


      
      
well, and plan to use good

      
      


      
      
varieties, and consider

      
      


      
      
irrigation if necessary, Roberts

      
      


      
      
said.  He added that proper

      
      


      
      
spray equipment is important.

      
      


      
      


      
      


      
      
More information about growing

      
      


      
      
vegetable crops can be found at

      
      


      
      
the county Extension offices.
