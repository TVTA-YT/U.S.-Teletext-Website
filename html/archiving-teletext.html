Archiving Teletext

How is teletext recovery still possible all these years later? Have a look for yourself!

Picture This...

Someone in the 1980s, let's say 1985, records their favorite program from CBS, NBC, or TBS onto their choice of videotape (Betamax or VHS). After the program, they might use that same tape to record other programs, but once it's full, onto the shelf it goes. What this person might not have realized is that besides recording a TV program, they recorded something else that they couldn't visibly see on their TV screen, something that has seemingly become forgotten here in the U.S.: teletext.

Wait... Really?

It's true! Many people will have transferred a videotape in their lives, whether it's a home movie or a TV broadcast. I (the creator of this site) have done my fair share of videotape transfers going back to 2011. Like most people who have transferred videotape, I assumed the only content on the tape was the programming content I was transferring. It wasn't until around 2025 when I realized just how much I was missing by transferring videotape the usual conventional way, which for me was transferring to a DVD recorder, then importing the files onto my computer.

How, Though?

In the days of analog TV, there was an unused portion of the video signal called the vertical blanking interval (VBI). TVs normally do not show the VBI, so many don't know it's there. The VBI can hold things such as CEA-608 closed captioning (sometimes called Line 21 captions since these captions were on Line 21 of the VBI), vertical interval timecode (VITC), test signals, and even data. I'm not going to go into the specifics of what the VBI is, so I'll link the Wikipedia article for that. There's also a great lecture video on YouTube uploaded in 2022 by the U.K.'s Centre for Computing History that discusses what teletext is and how it can be recovered from old videotapes.

When a program was recorded to videotape, not only was the program itself recorded, but the VBI was recorded as well. Whatever was present in the VBI at the time of the recording was preserved in the videotaped recording. This means that, if recording a program with Line 21 captions, those captions remained in the recording. Moreover, this means that if someone recorded a network with a teletext feed, such as TBS, the teletext was also preserved in the VBI.

Some high-profile teletext services include CBS's ExtraVision, NBC's NBC Teletext, and Keyfax/Electra, the previous two being included in TBS's VBI, with Electra replacing Keyfax in the mid 1980s. ExtraVision and NBC Teletext used a standard known as the North American Broadcast Teletext Standard (NABTS), while Keyfax/Electra use the pre-existing World System Teletext (WST) standard.

Wait, What Are Those?

Those are the different standards used here in the U.S.; Europe only used the WST standard. A big difference between the two standards concerned the graphical capabilities. WST relied heavily on text, NABTS relied on both text and graphics. To that point, the graphical capabilities of NABTS services exceeded WST services.

Do Capture Cards Work?

Yes, some capture cards are capable of capturing the VBI and therefore the teletext data. An up-to-date list of capable devices, as well as instructions for capturing the raw data is available from the Analog Video Wiki's page on Capturing VBI signals.

How Do I Get Started?

The simplest method for capturing raw VBI data from the playback of videotape recordings is by using an appropriate capture card as described in the Analog Video Wiki's page on Capturing VBI signals. It is important with this capture method that the playback VCR be connected directly to the capture device and not passed through other equipment such as DVD recorders, as these other devices can alter or remove the VBI data you are attempting to capture.

A more advanced way to preserve the VBI when transferring videotape is using the FM RF capture method and the VHS-Decode software. This method insures preservation of the entire video signal, including all lines of the VBI where teletext data is found. Detailed information on this method of videotape capture is available on the VHS-Decode wiki.

Once you have archived some VBI data with teletext, the decoding is performed using a software tool called Decode-Orc. This software, though part of the suite of software tools related to LD-Decode and VHS-Decode, can be used to decode VBI data captured either with the simple, conventional method or the advanced FM RF capture method. Most importantly for U.S. teletext archiving, Decode-Orc is currently the only software decoder that supports both NABTS and WST standards. The teletext part of the software, while officially released, is still under development. As more members of the tape archiving community use the software and discover bugs and other issues, the software will improve over time.

However, CBS and NBC are different; before assuming that any recording of programming from those two networks will automatically have teletext, there are a couple of important things to note.

1.) Local vs. National Programming

The national broadcast networks (ABC, CBS, FOX, NBC) do not provide a 24/7 schedule of programming. To make up for time when no programming from the network is being aired, affiliates fill these time slots with locally produced content, nationally syndicated content, and local news coverage. Even during network programming, stations insert local commercials and promotions.

It is our experience that most stations did not "bridge" the network VBI during local time and thus only broadcast teletext when carrying a network feed. Even during prime time, teletext data would drop off every time a station aired a local promo, commercial, or plug for its local news.

What this means is that the teletext services from CBS and NBC were not always being broadcast at all times by most affiliates while they were on air. Teletext data was relayed during all broadcast hours on CBS owned & operated (O&O) stations, stations that the broadcast networks, as the term suggests, owned and operated. NBC O&O stations did not provide this constant relay of teletext data as evidenced by the recordings we have so far examined. The five CBS O&Os of the 1980s were WCBS-TV New York, KNXT/KCBS-TV Los Angeles, WBBM-TV Chicago, WCAU-TV Philadelphia, and KMOX-TV St. Louis. These stations likely had consistent teletext information, as did the three affiliates that broadcast local Extravision: WBTV Charlotte, WIVB-TV Buffalo, and KSL-TV Salt Lake City. Note that CBS sold KMOX-TV in 1986, at which time it became KMOV, so we do not know if it continued to bridge the VBI. 2.) Local Affiliate Usage of the VBI

Not all local affiliates are alike. Differences in operating practices as well as the type of equipment used at each station can affect what signals ended up in each station's VBI. The images below illustrate how this can impact the teletext recovery process. These images are enlargements of the VBI taken from recordings of two different CBS affiliates of the 1980s: WHAS-TV, Louisville, KY and WBBM-TV, Chicago, IL.

To make spotting the difference clearer, outlines and labels have been added to each image to delineate different signals. In the WHAS-TV VBI, there are only five lines of teletext data when there should be eight for CBS ExtraVision. This is because WHAS-TV inserted three lines of VITS (vertical interval test signals) over top of three of the eight teletext lines. The VBI image from WBBM-TV shows that they were carrying the full eight lines of CBS ExtraVision teletext data.

The consequence of this is that the teletext data on the WHAS-TV recording does not represent the full data stream because it is missing three lines. This means that the complete ExtraVision magazine cannot be recovered from the WHAS-TV recording and that there are likely to be more corrupted or garbled pages in the recovery because the data is incomplete. In contrast, the CBS ExtraVision data in the WBBM-TV recording is complete and so the full magazine is recoverable.

Partial recoveries, while not ideal, are still better than no recovery at all. Unfortunately, some CBS and NBC affiliates either completely stripped or did not pass teletext data at all making recovery impossible from recordings of those stations. For an in-progress list of stations that stripped or altered the network teletext lines, visit this page.

How Can I Find Out?

The quickest and simplest way to determine whether a recording has teletext data in the VBI is to capture a short section of the recording's VBI using one of the capture devices listed on the Analog Video Wiki's page on Capturing VBI signals. The short VBI capture can then be examined using ffplay or Decode-Orc to see if teletext data is present. If you see at least several lines containing dense digital bits, you may have teletext!
