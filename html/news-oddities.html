NEW: WavePhore WaveTop data decoded

Add another VBI service to the list of service that have been decoded. For those uninformed, here's what WaveTop was, courtesy of this press release from Microsoft from October 8, 1997, where they announced the inclusion of WaveTop software in Windows 98:

WavePhore sends data over the broadcast signals of PBS National Datacast Inc.'s 264 PBS member stations, reaching more than 99 percent of television households in the United States. WaveTop, launching later this year, will be the first free, advertising-supported, nationwide data broadcast network delivering information, entertainment, and news for kids and adults. Several well-known content providers will have content available via WavePhore, including CBS SportsLine, PBS ONLINE, Quote.com, The Weather Channel, N2K's Music Boulevard, BarnesandNoble.com, NECX, People, TIME, Entertainment Weekly, Money, FORTUNE and Sports Illustrated for Kids.

Essentially, WaveTop was a way to access a limited version of the Internet. WaveTop has been on our list of services to decode for about a month, and now, we've finally got some examples from WaveTop. The decoded data only consists of HTML pages with images and GIF files. This data, though, was decoded from a 10-minute sample source from a KET recording dated late 1998. Like with StarSight, having a longer capture likely means more complete data and more data overall.

A page for WaveTop will come as soon as we have more data to work with. Implementing WaveTop pages on the site requires some thought from me as I need to figure out a way to present the links.

Written by: Monique Sammie Brie

NEW: ExtraVision sample from March 15, 1983 – oldest known NABTS sample

NABTS without geometric graphics? Only eight colors? Blasphemy! Nope, it's real, and one of my latest sample proves it. Dated March 15, 1983, and recorded alongside Gone with the Wind from CBS (WBBM-TV), it provides the first look at ExtraVision in the weeks ahead of its national launch on April 4.

In many ways, this sample is primordial. When CBS launched ExtraVision, it did not yet have the equipment necessary to originate what were called alphageometric graphics — that is, graphics that are drawn from shape instructions instead of blocks. These were NABTS's selling point, especially to advertisers concerned about the fidelity of brand logos. CBS did not begin broadcasting alphageometric teletext until May 24, after it had installed the necessary equipment from the French firm VSA.

So what you get is alphamosaic graphics instead. It feels a bit like alphageometric NABTS had a baby with World System Teletext: blocky graphics, limited colors, but the pages are identifiably ExtraVision in their style and sensibility.

Aside from the news, sports, finance, and weather information EV transmitted throughout its life, as well as CBS schedule information and captions (but no NewsCaps), here is also a variety of content not seen in later years. Communications Daily is already providing pages of news, but there is a travel section with pages of interesting events in March and lowest airfares from New York to various US cities. Entertainment offers shows on Broadway and in Las Vegas and Atlantic City. And there's a chart of the most popular video games, led by Activision's Pitfall.

Equally significant is the short page EF0: "TDF-CBS". TDF almost certainly refers to Télédiffusion de France, which at the time was the broadcast services company to French TV. It was active on the Antiope and NABTS scenes.

Sample courtesy of Monique Sammi Brie.

NEW: Scrambled SportScreen and other sports pages on later Electra samples have been decoded

On later Electra samples, currently only noticed in 1992 samples, new pages were added to the TEMPO magazine, which starts at P200. These pages were from a service called SportScreen and featured sport scores and betting lines. This was a subscription service, meaning that a decoder was required to view the pages. What this in turn means is that the pages, while the can technically be viewed, would show only random text and mosaic characters, no readable text. Another section was called "Mel Stewart's Picks," and had similar content as SportScreen; however, these pages were scrambled differently. On these pages, you could see that there were actual words and sentences on the page, but they used random letters to obscure the words (e.g. "KDQLJKDI CJJQGDII GDVNEQGDII DKA...").

A couple days ago (as of this post), I began trying to figure out how these pages could be decoded. Here's what I discovered.

SportScreen Pages

I was initially stuck concerning the SportScreen pages, but while editing an Electra sample dated December 13, 1992, I noticed something interesting. The editor has a section that shows the hex value of the character where the cursor is placed (e.g. 20 for a space). I normally don't pay attention to that, but when I moved the cursor around in an empty row on one of the SportScreen pages, I saw that the hex values didn't stay at 20, but they kept changing, which I found odd. I then took note of the hex value I was seeing and came up with this:

21 00 23 24 25 1e 26 2a 28 29 29 28 2a 26 1e 25 24 23 00 21 21 00 23 24 25 1e 26 2a 28 29 29 28 2a 26 1e 25 24 23 00 21

This meant that these weren't ordinary spaces, but different characters and control codes. It turns out the text was colored black to blend in with the background. Coloring that text produced the characters occupying the rows (control codes ignored):

! £$% &*())(*& %$£ !! £$% &*())(*& £$%

I was initially unsure of what to make of this, but the thing I noticed was that "blank" rows all had this same line of characters, so I knew it had to mean something. I used some online calculators to try to figure out if I could try to get something useful out of that line of characters. To help with that, I used this website to help figure out the encryption scheme. I was still getting nothing because I didn't have a decryption key, so I had to think harder about how to find this key. While examining various encrypted and standard (non-SportScreen pages) rows, I thought about the thing I mentioned earlier about normal spaces having a hex value of 20. Maybe that came into view somehow. I researched some of the available encryption/decryption ciphers on the website linked earlier and found one that might match what I'm looking for: XOR.

If I wanted to try the XOR decryption, I still needed a key. In the website linked above, when applying an XOR calculation, there's an input for a key; of course, I didn't have a key, so I just tried inputting a couple different hex values to convert that row of characters into a different hex string. One of these character was 20, the hex code for a space. This was the breakthrough.

Using 20 as the XOR key converted the hex above to this hex string:

01 20 03 04 05 3e 06 0a 08 09 09 08 0a 06 3e 05 04 03 20 01 01 20 03 04 05 3e 06 0a 08 09 09 08 0a 06 3e 05 04 03 20 01

I thought that this might be they key I'm looking for. To try it out, I typed the hex values from a line that has encrypted text and converted it to Base64. To my surprise, it worked. Take a look at some examples below (spaces and control codes ignored in final output for this table).

Raw Hex Base 64
05 3d 04 4a 47 7f 26 46 41 47 4c 5b 2a 40 71 57 24 32 12 2e 30 12 23 24 2e 1e 3b 2a 40 48 45 4e 2a 26 0f 55 45 33 10 2b NBA LINES FOR 12/12 + = HALF 1PA00*

The control codes determined the color of a row. For example, in the converted hex, 05 became 04, the value used for a blue row, and 07 became 06, the value used for a cyan row.

I then worked on adding a decoder to the JS for both the WST teletext editor and viewer. After taking a day or so to do that, it's now available on both pages. An example is below.

Mel Stewart Pages

After tackling the SportScreen pages, there was another section of pages I needed to look at in the same 1992 Electra sample. These pages were part of a section called "Mel Stewart's Picks". This section was easier to work with than the SportScreen section.

These pages use a different decryption scheme than the SportScreen pages. Unlike the SportScreen pages, you can clearly make out words and sentences on these pages, but each word is a mixture of random alphanumeric characters. Let's focus on one of the first sentences on the first page, specifically the first 2 words: CJW QHE GEVQ PID½V JC QHE AD½ FDII?

You can clearly see there's something there, but it's not decipherable. A discussion about these pages happened in the Teletext Discord group at the beginning of September. I went back and read through the messages. It appeared that these pages used letter substitution. One user provided a few examples (only 6 listed here):

  • C → F
  • J → O
  • W → R
  • Q → T
  • H → unchanged
  • E → unchanged

According to this user, the first line read, in part, FOR THE.... While we had part of the first line, there were still about 20 more rows of text to be decoded, and we still didn't know (or have a list of) known letter substitutions. After reading those messages, I decided to figure this out as well.

It turns out that the cipher is a letter substitution cipher, specifically a variation of XOR 5. First, let's start with the raw hex values for a row on the first page, row 4:

0d 20 20 20 20 20 20 20 20 20 20 4d 45 49 20 56 51 45 52 44 57 51 22 56 20 50 4c 46 4e 56 20 20 20 20 20 20 20 20 20 20

As you can see, there are a lot of 20 values, those being normal, unencrypted spaces, so no special key is required. It's just a matter of figuring out which characters go where. What made this interesting was that some letters, such as E and H, did not change. This likely meant there were other characters that didn't change, which added a bit of complexity. In the end, this was how the cipher was decoded:

To find out what characters go where, this first involves converting the hex values to binary. If we take the hex values for row 4 (above), ignoring the values for spaces and control codes, and convert them to binary, we get this:

01001101 01000101 01001001 01010110 01010001 01000101 01010010 01000100 01010111 01010001 00100010 01010110 01010000 01001100 01000110 01001110 01010110

To decipher the letters, this will involve bit-swapping, specifically looking at the last 3 bits of each binary value. Let's look at the first 3 binary values in the binary above: 01001101 01000101 01001001. The key thing to know is that binary values that end with 000 or 101 do not change. As you can see, the first 2 values end with 101, so these do not change. The binary values correspond to the letters M and E, meaning those letters in the encrypted output do not change. The last value, however, does change.

In the encrypted output, this value corresponds to I. Using bit-swapping, we can figure out the true letter that goes in its place. To do this, we only need to swap the first and third bits, like so:

001 → 100

Every bit before the last 3 bits stays the same, so after the swapping, we have the value 01001100, which corresponds to L. This means that the first word in row 4 is MEL.

Let's look at another example from the same line. This time, the second word, written in the encrypted output as "VQERDWQ"V". Here's the binary (without spaces and control codes) for those characters:

01010110 01010001 01000101 01010010 01000100 01010111 01010001 00100010 01010110

Remember, if the last 3 bits end with 000 or 101, the bits do not change. That happens for only 1 character, which is once again E. We use the same method to convert these bytes, only swapping the first and third bits.

Original Binary Original Letter Converted Binary Converted Letter
01010110 V 01010011 S
01010001 Q 01010100 T
01000101 E NO CHANGE E
01010010 R 01010111 W
01000100 D 01000001 A
01010111 W 01010010 R
01010001 G 01010100 T
00100010 " 00100111 '
01010110 V 01010011 S

Converting the entirety of row 4 using this method, it produces: MEL STEWART'S PICKS. The same 3-bit substitution works across each Mel Stewart page. After figuring that out, all that was left was to add it to the JS used for the editor and viewer, which is now available on both pages. An example is below.

NEW: New pages for editing and viewing WST teletext samples; new way to convert T34 files to T42

This new is a few days old, but I'm just getting around to adding this article to this page. Anyway, we previously had no way to view our WST (T34) samples in an actual software-based viewer, nor was there a way to modify the pages in the teletext stream to correct errors, such as spelling mistakes or garbled and/or incomplete graphics. Now, we've got both.

A New Teletext Viewer

VHS-Teletext, software tools for decoding and viewing T42 teletext files, has been around for about 10 years now, but us here in NTSC countries couldn't use it because it only worked with T42 files, not T34 files. With the conversion script, we can now use these tools, and that's what I did after producing the first T42 files from samples I had. The tools include recording the VBI from a videotape, deconvolving the VBI file to a T42 file, examining headers, splitting the capture into different services, displaying all copies of a single page, squashing duplicate pages, generating HTML pages, and an interactive viewer that allows you to view the entire teletext stream at once by navigating to different pages.

The interactive viewer caught my eye, so I tried it, and it was great. I could simply type a page number and it would go to that page. I knew then that I wanted to add something like that to this website, but I wasn't sure how. I then began figuring that out.

This, however, would be harder than the teletext editor because the code was written in Python, a language I have no familiarity with. If I wanted to add the viewer, it would need to be rewritten in JS. The Python code was only 215 lines long, so it was quite short, but I knew JS would require a lot more since this would be added to the website; the Python viewer is its own standalone application. I looked through the code and, as best as I could understand, noted what each function block did. With my layout created, I could start working on the editor, but I didn't know what JS method correspond to the existing Python methods.

Disclaimer: I had some assistance from AI tools to help me translate existing Python methods, such as setstyle, _pos, getmaxyx, and inputtmp into equivalent JS methods since I had no idea how some of the Python methods worked. Once these translations were figured ou and noted, I began to work on an equivalent web-based viewer that not only kept the same functionality, but added a bit more to it. This took a few days to work on and test,

On September 27, the teletext viewer was made public. The viewer is not linked in the navigation menu; to view a sample inside the editor, view available results from any WST service (DaTaVizion, Electra, Keyfax, SSS Teletext, Virtext, and Wisconsin Infotext) and look for the teletext icon (a TV-style icon with 3 horizontal bars inside) and click on it. This icon is within a new table column titled "View Teletext Sample". Here's a current example from Electra dated December 13, 1992.

A big thanks to the people that worked on VHS-Teletext for helping give me inspiration and ideas needed to work on the viewer!

A New Teletext Editor

We've now got a teletext viewer, now we needed an editor. Once again, existing code came to the rescue.

About a week and a half ago, I, along with someone else, began to figure out how how to incorporate an editor into this website. I had the idea but needed some help with it. That's when I was pointed to this GitHub repository. In the repository was code used by the Teletext Archive website for a T42 (PAL-based WST) teletext editor (big thanks to them for making the code available!). I had seen a screenshot of the editor days before and really loved the design and its overall function and wanted to add something like that to this website. I previously didn't know the code was on GitHub.

With that, we looked through it and saw that the code was written in C# (C-sharp). This made things a bit easier for me because I had some familiarity with C# since I took classes in college where C# was used. However, the editor as it was couldn't be used because the C# worked in conjunction with a Windows Form. My primary OS is macOS, so I didn't have a way to test the form except uploading the code to WASM (Web Assembly), which I've never used before. While all the code was there, something else needed to be done.

The decision was to rewrite the code in JavaScript (JS). Not only does this skip Web Assembly and Windows Forms, meaning less extra steps, but it also means that the editor would work in any browser on any OS. After looking through the code and reading it's comments, it was time to build the editor.

This took about 6 or so days to work on. Converting C# to JS wasn't all that difficult. There was vigorous testing of the editor during the building process. I enlisted the help of a couple others to help test the editor and report any bugs they found. I managed to keep almost everything the original editor did. On September 29, the editor was finally made available; it can be found under the "Other Pages" navigation dropdown option. Please note that the editor is not available on mobile devices.

Converting T34 to T42

We've now got an editor and viewer, but something else was still missing.

While some modifications were applied to the original teletext editor code, one thing remained unchanged: the editor can only read T42 (PAL) files. This was something I decided to keep even though none of us have T42 files, only T34 files. Let me explain why I didn't change that.

The original plan was going to be making the edited code read T34 files, but that would've required rewriting a lot of the code. I then had a thought: keep the code as is, but create a separate script to convert the T34 NTSC files to T42 PAL files. After more perusing, there was another GitHub repository with code used on the Teletext Archive website. I forked the code to my GitHub account to look through it. In one of the files, again mostly C#, was code that read a T42 file. Using this information, plus some information about T34 packets I had previously saved, I worked on creating a new conversion script.

The sole purpose of this script would be to take an existing T34 file and convert it to T42 – that's all. None of the page data itself would be touched in any way. This was the quickest script to work on. I decided to not use JS for this and instead used C# because this script would be hosted on GitHub, not included as part of the website. That script is complete and works on all major OSes; the script can be found here. After a converted T42 file is created, it can be imported into the teletext editor.

Thanks Are in Order

I couldn't have added these pages without the help of preexisting code, so a big thank you to the developers of VHS-Teletext and the Teletext Archive website for making the code publicly available!

Today, another milestone in decoding VBI data has been achieved: data from Silent Radio has officially been decoded. This has been on our to-do list for a few weeks, the same list that initially included StarSight.

Silent Radio information is... presented in six-minute cycles, containing 11 news, sports and weather reports and five advertising messages. When the service goes national, the information will be fed to affiliated broadcast stations via satellite.

StarSight EPG data has been decoded; new page coming soon

StarSight EPG data has been decoded

For the last few days, myself and a few others have been researching information about a VBI service called National Datacast (NDI), which was provided by PBS. In the early days of NDI, one of the services it provided was EPG (electronic program guide) listings from a provider known as StarSight.

What spurred this research was the presence of teletext-like data in the VBI seen in recordings from KCET (Los Angeles, CA), a PBS affiliate. Attempts to decode this data in Decode-Orc using both the NABTS and Teletext sinks produced no results, so we wanted to figure out what this data could be. At first, we suspected that it might be another teletext standard known as Antiope, created in France. However, as we dug deeper, we began to suspect that this data was possibly related to NDI. With that, I and the others began trying to find as much information as we could about NDI and what standards it used. This information would be sent to the developer of a custom fork of the CC_Decoder project.

Let's move forward to September 14. Initial tests with submitted VBI samples conducted by the developer mentioned earlier showed that StarSight data was discovered in the VBI data, which came from recordings from the statewide PBS network KET in Kentucky; these recordings were from late 1998. Soon after, we started to get our first results showing decoded StarSight data, data rendered in HTML, but much of it was incomplete, likely due to the short runtimes of the samples, meaning longer samples would be needed to get a better sense of what data was being transmitted.

Soon after, updated code was published to the fork on GitHub for testing. Yesterday, September 16, I transferred two hours of data recorded from KCET on May 3, 1994. I had initially submitted a ten minute sample about a week ago, but no data was found, likely because the sample was too short. After decoding the sample and running it through CC_Decoder, I opened the HTML file and saw a very populated table containing rows for program title, channel ID, days of the week, and program start times, to name a few. This is the most extensive StarSight data we have as of now. The VBI sample has been sent to the CC_Decoder fork developer so more can be done with the existing code since this sample has much more data than any of the previous samples.

Coming Soon: New page for StarSight data

Now that we have this newly decoded data, it has been suggested that there should be a way to view this data. Granted, HTML files are already generated from CC_Decoder, but like with the teletext and text samples, there should be a central place to view this data. That's why I'll soon be adding a new page for viewing this decoded StarSight data. This page is currently under construction. There will be a lot of backend logic to make this page work with the provided JSON (JavaScript Object Notation) files, so I cannot provide an exact date as to when this page will be available. I'm hoping it'll be available in the next few days or so. Once it's available, it'll be under the "Other Teletext Pages" navigation dropdown link.

Shutdown date for KET AGTEXT confirmed

One of the text (non-teletext) services we have been archiving on the site is Kentucky Educational television's (KET) AGTEXT (Agricultural Text) service. This was a service provided by KET in cooperation with the University of Kentucky's College of Agriculture. The service provided daily updates about daily agricultural news, such as weather reports, agricultural news and information, and agriculture prices. There isn't much information about AGTEXT overall, so we had neither a launch or shutdown. As of today, we now have an approximate launch date and an exact shutdown date.

According to an AGTEXT sample dated November 7, 1998, the latest sample we have so far, KET announced that AGTEXT would shut down on January 31, 1999. The launch date for AGTEXT was sometime in December 1983. Read KET's statement below, which comes from that November 1998 sample.

AGTEXT INFORMATION SERVICE TO BE DISCONTINUED

After a long run...the Agtext service will be discontinued on January 31, 1999. The service, which started during Dec. 1983, was a cooperative effort between Kentucky Educational Television (KET) and the UK College of Agriculture.

It's purpose was to utilize the latest technology to improve the dissemination of agricultural-related information to Kentucky residents...especially farm families.

With the advent and expansion of the Internet, technology has rapidly moved forward, far beyond the capability of the Agtext service, according to Mr. Tom Priddy, director of the service since its inception.

TBS continued broadcasting teletext through 1997

When we first started decoding samples of Electra from tapes with content recorded from TBS, we assumed, based on the information already out there, that Electra shut down sometime in 1993. Then, in mid-August, a submission was sent in showing stock pages dated December 1995, pages exported from a teletext stream transmitted by TBS.

We first assumed that this was Electra and that we had evidence to refute the claim that Electra shut down in 1993, like what we had discovered with CBS's ExtraVision and NBC Teletext. However, especially after finding the page about Electra's shut down (see August 26, 2026, update), that proved that those pages weren't Electra per se. Rather, these were part of a group of pages from TEMPO, more specifically, stock listings. Moreover, this service was called SSS (Satellite Syndicated Systems) Teletext.

All that leads me to September 7, 2026. While a submission was received yesterday (September 6) with pages decoded from a recording dated 1996, screenshots were posted to the Teletext Discord group showing the index page of SSS Teletext dated March 16, 1997. This is the most recent teletext example we have seen so far, and honestly, quite a surprise to us. To add, the "SSS Teletext" name was not present on the index page, so we're not sure if the teletext service changed names or if SSS decided to just drop the name on-screen. In any case, seeing teletext data from as recently as 1997 was interesting.

NEW: First known example of non-O&O CBS affiliate passing through teletext during local programming

We previously assumed that only O&O (owned & operated) CBS affiliates, such as WCBS (New York, NY) and others passed through CBS's ExtraVision service at all times. Indeed, we have samples from station such as the aforementioned WCBS and KDFW (Dallas, TX) to back up that claim. Today, we discovered the first example of a non-O&O affiliate passing through ExtraVision during local programming.

This affiliate is WCPX (now WGMK - Orlando, FL). One of the newest ExtraVision samples submitted today was dated June 30, 1986. The data was captured during an airing of the original Star Trek series. However, there were no records of that program airing nationally on that day. It then hit us: this was a local, syndicated broadcast.

WCPX passed through the full teletext stream during the program, which is significant. This, of course, proves that some non-O&O stations did choose to pass through the entire teletext stream during local programming. We're actively trying to find out if any other affiliates did this same thing.

August 28, 2026

Electra shutdown date confirmed; TEMPO stock pages continued through 1995

We can now confirm the definitive shutdown date for Electra: June 11, 1993. This news was discovered from a decoded Electra page found on a TBS broadcast of an episode of Perry Mason from March 22, 1993.

While Electra ceased operations in 1993, stock listings provided by TEMPO, however, did not. TEMPO data was broadcast alongside Electra, and even after Electra's shutdown, TEMPO data continued to be transmitted as late as December 1995.

CBS ExtraVision did not shut down in 1988 CBS ExtraVision did not shut down in 1988

You may have read before from various places that ExtraVision shut down in 1988. However, what if I were to say that wasn't the case? Have a look for yourself...

Images courtesy of Zaroff's Video Archives.

Those are recently decoded pages that come from a VHS recording from February 28, 1990; you can even see the full date in one of the photos. Let me add that there are a lot of decoding issues with these pages. Part of that is likely due to current limitations with Decode-Orc. As I've stated elsewhere, the software is still in development, so there may likely be less of those issues as development progresses. Another possible issue is that the videotape these pages were sourced from was recorded in EP/SLP mode, which can make obtaining cleaner decodes more difficult due to the lower quality.
